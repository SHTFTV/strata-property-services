Fireplace Upgrades in a Strata: What Approvals Are Needed?

Swapping a fireplace in a strata unit usually needs more than a contractor's quote. Here's the approval path under BC's Strata Property Act and the gas rules, and what councils should ask for before work starts.

November 27, 2026 · StrataPropertyServices.com Editorial · 5 min read

Gas fireplace upgrades in a BC strata commonly need several separate approvals before work starts: written approval from the strata (usually through council, under the strata's alteration bylaws), a gas permit taken out by a licensed gas contractor, and often a signed alteration and indemnity agreement. If the job changes common property in a significant way, such as a new vent through an exterior wall or a new chimney, the Strata Property Act can require a 3/4 vote at a general meeting as well. The exact rules depend on your strata's registered bylaws, so councils and owners should start there.

Step 1: Check which bylaws apply

Start with the strata's current registered bylaws and the strata plan. Under section 120 of the Strata Property Act, the Standard Bylaws apply except where different bylaws have been filed. A building can therefore have a combination of standard provisions and amendments; do not rely on another strata's bylaws.

Under Standard Bylaw 5, an owner needs the strata's written approval before altering a strata lot in ways that involve the building's structure, its exterior, chimneys, stairs, balconies or other attached features, common property inside the lot, or parts of the lot the strata must insure. Standard Bylaw 6 requires written approval before any change to common property, including limited common property.

Standard Bylaw 5 also has a bare-land-strata exception. A fireplace upgrade in a conventional apartment or townhouse strata can touch several regulated elements at once: the vent or chimney, an exterior wall, the framing around the firebox, and fixtures the strata insures.

Step 2: Is it a "significant change" to common property?

Section 71 of the Strata Property Act says the strata must not make a significant change in the use or appearance of common property unless it's approved by a 3/4 vote at an annual or special general meeting, or is needed immediately for safety or to prevent significant damage. An owner proposal affecting common property needs review under the applicable alteration bylaws and statutory requirements. Ask the strata to assess significance before committing to the work; a contractor cannot supply the strata vote or approval.

Examples that may cross the line, depending on the building:

  • A new direct vent termination on a visible exterior wall.
  • A new chimney or flue through the roof.
  • Removing a masonry chimney or changing the building's exterior appearance.

A replacement in an existing location still needs review of the actual scope. Do not assume the old vent is compatible with the new appliance, or that a similar-looking installation needs no approval. The licensed contractor should confirm the manufacturer's venting requirements, while the strata considers the property and appearance changes. Obtain advice when the approval threshold is uncertain.

Step 3: The gas permit and who can do the work

Technical Safety BC's permit guidance includes gas fireplace installation and replacement among work requiring a permit. It states that strata owners cannot obtain its homeowner gas permits and must hire a licensed contractor. The contractor should confirm the correct permit issuer and requirements for the address, fuel and system. Municipal delegation is not identical for every gas installation, so use the current gas jurisdiction guidance.

Councils should request the permit number and the relevant completion and assessment records. Under Technical Safety BC's assessment process, a declaration can be accepted without a remote or physical inspection. Permit issuance, an accepted compliance declaration and a completed physical inspection are different records; ask for the record actually issued.

Step 4: Alteration and indemnity agreements

Standard Bylaw 5 says the strata must not unreasonably withhold approval for a strata lot alteration, but can require the owner to agree in writing to take responsibility for expenses relating to it. A written alteration and indemnity agreement can record that responsibility.

Keep alteration agreements with the strata records. Section 59 of the Act requires the Information Certificate to disclose agreements under which the owner takes responsibility for alteration expenses. Whether a particular obligation binds a later purchaser depends on the agreement, bylaws and applicable law; do not assume disclosure alone settles every succession question. Owners and purchasers should have the actual documents reviewed.

Tips for owners

  • Ask before you buy. Get council's answer on venting and appearance before ordering a unit.
  • Plan for the vote. If a 3/4 vote may be needed, the timeline is set by the next general meeting, not the contractor's schedule.
  • Talk to your neighbours. Vent terminations near balconies and windows are a common source of complaints.
  • Keep the paperwork. Approvals, permits and the indemnity agreement will matter when you sell.

Tips for councils

  • Be consistent. Use the same application form and criteria for every owner so decisions are defensible.
  • Respond in writing, with reasons, and within a reasonable time.
  • Apply the correct approval rule. Standard Bylaw 5 prohibits unreasonable withholding of the approval it covers. Check the registered bylaws and distinguish strata-lot work from common-property work.
  • Get advice on whether a change is significant before deciding a vote isn't needed.

If the proposal is to replace gas with an electric fireplace, identify the gas decommissioning, electrical work and enclosure changes separately. A gas permit is not a universal permit for every electric appliance. The relevant qualified trades and permit authorities should confirm the scope, and the strata should review any affected common property or insured building elements.

This article is general information, not legal advice. For a specific situation, check your strata's registered bylaws and speak with a strata lawyer or your property manager.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need strata approval to replace my gas fireplace?

Usually yes. Under the Standard Bylaws, owners need written approval before alterations involving chimneys, the building exterior, structure or parts the strata insures, and many stratas have similar or stricter bylaws.

When does a fireplace upgrade need a 3/4 vote?

A proposed fireplace alteration that significantly changes the use or appearance of common property may require a three-quarter vote under section 71, subject to the Act's conditions and emergency exception. Ask the strata to assess the actual proposal before ordering or installing equipment.

Can a strata owner get a homeowner gas permit?

Technical Safety BC states that strata owners cannot obtain its homeowner gas permits and must hire a licensed contractor. The contractor should confirm the correct permitting jurisdiction for the actual installation.

Does an indemnity agreement transfer to a new owner?

That requires review of the particular agreement, registered bylaws and applicable law. Section 59 requires disclosure of alteration-expense agreements in the Information Certificate; disclosure alone should not be treated as a universal answer about a later purchaser's obligations.
